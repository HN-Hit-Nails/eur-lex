4.2.     Geographic Market Definition

4.2.1    The Commission’s past decisional practice
(39)     In its past decisional practice, the Commission has considered that the geographic
         scope of the markets for parcel delivery services, and its possible segments is
         likely national. Contracts for parcel deliveries are mostly negotiated at national
         level for both domestic and international deliveries, and parcel delivery
         companies need a significant national presence in each country (e.g.,
         infrastructure, client contracts, sales force) to meaningfully compete in that
         country. The Commission also noted that there may be a tendency towards
         broader markets. (39)

4.2.2    The Notifying Parties’ views
(40)     The Notifying Parties do not dispute the Commission’s past decisional practice of
         the geographic market definition. In relation to the geographic scope of the OOH
         market for the delivery of parcels, the Notifying Parties consider it to be national
         in scope, and that it should not be segmented by catchment area. (40)

4.2.3    The Commission’s assessment
(41)     The Commission’s investigation largely confirmed its past decisional practice that
         the relevant geographic market for parcel delivery services is national in scope.
(42)     More specifically, a majority of competitors that expressed a view during the
         market investigation, agree that the relevant product markets for the provision of
         parcel delivery services are all national in scope. (41) Nonetheless, regarding
         cross-border segments, including the intra-EEA and extra-EEA delivery of
         parcels, a majority of competitors that expressed a view consider competitive
         conditions to be homogenous at regional level or EEA-wide. (42)
(43)     Customers that expressed a view, on the other hand, provided differing responses
         depending on the relevant product market segments (of the market for the delivery
         of parcels), with overall results being mixed and inconclusive, split between
         national, regional and EEA level.The overall majority of respondents agree that
         the relevant geographic market for parcel delivery services is national in scope.

4.2.4    Conclusion
(44)     The Commission considers that the geographic scope of the markets for the
         provision of parcel delivery services is national because parcel delivery
         companies need a significant national presence in each country to be able to
         meaningfully compete in that country. Having a national network, including hubs,
         agencies and line hauling capabilities, is a significant barrier to entry also in cross-
         border segments.

5.      LEGAL FRAMEWORK
(46)    Under Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation, the Commission must assess
        whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective
        competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular
        because of the creation or strengthening of a dominant position. When assessing
        concentrations between actual or potential competitors, the Commission
        investigates whether such concentrations result in adverse ‘non-coordinated
        effects’ and/or in ‘coordinated effects’, as described in the Guidelines on the
        assessment of horizontal mergers under the Merger Regulation (the ‘Horizontal
        Merger Guidelines’). (43)
(47)    Non-coordinated effects occur due to the removal of an important competitive
        constraint to the parties to a concentration, typically the loss of competition
        between the firms in the concentration. (44) To assess the competitive constraints
        faced by the merging parties, the Horizontal Merger Guidelines include certain
        factors that the Commission uses to measure the likelihood and magnitude of non-
        coordinated effects. Such factors include: (i) the market shares of the parties; (ii)
        whether the merging firms are close competitors; (iii) whether there are limited
        possibilities for customers to switch to the parties’ competitors; (iv) whether the
        parties’ competitors could increase supply if prices increase; (v) whether the
        merged entity would be able to hinder its competitors’ expansion; and (vi)
        whether the merger removes a competitor that is stronger than its market shares
        suggest. By considering these and other factors, the Commission assesses whether
        a potential increase in market power is likely to lead to a significant impediment
        of effective competition, namely because of the creation or the strengthening of a
        dominant position.
(48)    Coordinated effects occur when a merger is changing the nature of competition in
        such a way that firms that previously were not coordinating their behaviour, are
        now significantly more likely to coordinate and raise prices or otherwise harm
        effective competition. (45) As coordinated effects are not relevant for the
        assessment of the Transaction, they will not be further discussed in this Decision.

         as to the compatibility of the Transaction with the internal market.
(121)    First, it is not up to the supplier to decide whether customers purchase multiple
         services of theirs. While a majority of customers that expressed a view in the
         investigation mentioned that they typically purchase different parcel delivery
         services as a bundled service from the same provider, (147) customers also
         multisource for their parcel delivery service’s needs, as explained above in
         paragraph (68). This allows especially medium-sized and large customers to adopt
         a multi-carrier sourcing strategy, allocating volumes across several providers,
         services, channels and countries, which allows customers to derisk their supply
         chain, as well as selecting the most convenient logistics provider for specific parts
         of their business. In particular, some of the Parties’ main customers are large
         international players, such as […], which have sophisticated procurement teams
         that can exert a degree of countervailing buyer power.
(122)    Second, there is only […] between the customer bases of InPost and FedEx. The
         Parties do not have more than […] common customers among their top fifty
         customers by revenue and volume on every affected market, which indicates that
         the Parties are not close commercial partners for the same client base, making any
         bundling strategy less relevant. (148)
