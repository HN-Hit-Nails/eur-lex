6.2.    Legal framework
(37)    Article 2(2) and (3) of the Merger Regulation require the Commission to assess
        whether a proposed concentration would significantly impede effective
        competition in the internal market or in a substantial part of it, in particular
        through the creation or strengthening of a dominant position.
(38)    The Horizontal Merger Guidelines (39) distinguish two main ways in which
        mergers between actual or potential competitors on the same relevant market may
        significantly impede effective competition, namely non-coordinated effects and
        coordinated effects.
(39)    Non-coordinated effects may significantly impede effective competition by
        eliminating the competitive constraint imposed by one merging party on the other,
        as a result of which the merged entity would have increased market power without
        resorting to coordinated behaviour. According to recital 25 of the Merger
        Regulation, a significant impediment to effective competition can result from the
        anticompetitive effects of a concentration even if the merged entity would not
        have a dominant position on the market concerned. In this regard, the Horizontal
        Merger Guidelines consider not only the direct loss of competition between the
        merging firms, but also the reduction in competitive pressure on non-merging
        firms in the same market that could be brought about by the merger. (40)
(40)    The Horizontal Merger Guidelines list a number of factors, which may influence
        the rise of substantial non-coordinated effects from a merger, such as the large
        market shares of the merging firms, the fact that the merging firms are close
        competitors, the limited possibilities for customers to switch suppliers, or the fact
        that the merger would eliminate an important competitive force. The list of factors
        also applies if a merger would create or strengthen a dominant position or would
        otherwise significantly impede effective competition due to non-coordinated
        effects. Furthermore, not all of those factors need to be present to make significant
        non-coordinated effects likely and the list itself is not exhaustive. (41)

(41)      The Parties submit that the Transaction does not give rise to concerns. In the

(52)      The market investigation indicated that market entry is not feasible based on low

          decommissioned will likely result in multiple new entrants in the medium term,
          including AJW Group, Turkish Technic, GA Telesis, and entrants from
          China. (53) It is predicted that as the current A350 global fleet ages and new A350
          aircraft are sold, the demand for CMS for A350 will continuously grow.

          decommissioned, a second-hand market for A350 spare parts will emerge and
          develop (based on spare parts of the decommissioned A350) which will facilitate
          the entry of CMS for A350 with access to cheaper spare parts. (54) One example
          of such a development concerns the transition to the newer version of A320neo
          that has triggered a release of spare parts of the older version into the secondary
          markets. (55)

(56)      The Commission has assessed the Parties’ submission on efficiencies on a no-

          efficiency claims in line with paragraph 77 of the Horizontal Merger

6.5.2.    The Parties’ views
(57)      The Parties expect that the Transaction will result in efficiencies. The Parties
          submit that the JV will enable the Parties to optimise their combined pool of spare
          parts, resulting in lower costs and a broader spare parts base (‘pool synergies’) as
          well as combine their geographic footprint, resulting in reduced transportation
          costs, lower lead times and a reduced environmental impact (‘geographic
          footprint synergies’), among other claimed efficiencies. (60)
(58)      Specifically, the Parties claim that pool synergies will result from jointly
          managing their inventories, decreasing the required investment in spare parts per
          aircraft in comparison to a scenario where both Parties provide their services on
          a standalone basis [planned cost reductions per aircraft]. They further claim that
          a combined pool would increase the probability that a spare part is available when
          needed and therefore reduces instances where customers would need to wait for
          spare parts. Consequently, the JV would significantly reduce aircraft-on-ground
          (‘AOG’) events by improving the timeliness of CMS. Airlines and consumers
          would benefit from increased aircraft availability and reduced schedule delays.

          JV to organise both outbound (i.e. from pool to customer) and inbound logistics
          (i.e. from customer to repair shop) from the best-positioned location, resulting in
          reduced transportation costs, lower lead times and a smaller environmental
          impact. Specifically, the Parties claim that [cost reductions for CMS]. They
          further claim that the JV could potentially generate a substantial AOG cost saving
          amounting to [cost reductions for AOG]. Finally, the JV would reduce airlines’
          environmental compliance costs for transport-related emissions and align with the
          environmental criteria in the commercial and procurement decisions of a growing
          number of airlines that adhere to public decarbonisation strategies.

6.5.3.    The Commission’s assessment
(60)      As explained in the Horizontal and Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, the
          Commission considers any substantiated efficiency claim in the overall
          assessment of a merger, where it may decide, as a consequence of the efficiencies
          that the merger brings about, that there are no grounds for declaring the merger
          incompatible with the common market pursuant to Article 2(3) of the Merger
          Regulation. (61) It is possible that efficiencies brought about by a merger
          counteract the effects on competition and in particular the potential harm to
          consumers that it might otherwise have. (62) However, there is no presumption
          that all concentrations give rise to efficiencies. (63) The Parties are required to
          provide, in due time, all information necessary for the assessment of efficiency
          claims. For the Commission to take such claims into account, the efficiencies must
          be verifiable, merger-specific, and result in benefits for consumers. (64)
(61)      The Commission considers that both pool synergies and geographic footprint
          synergies, as presented by the Parties, appear, prima facie, to satisfy at least two
          of the three conditions required for the Commission to accept efficiency claims.
          The proposed efficiencies appear, prima facie, to be merger-specific and unlikely
          to arise from the merging parties’ standalone or mere contractual arrangements
          between the merging parties or third parties. Should they materialise, such
          efficiencies could, in principle, result in consumer benefits in the form of lower
          CMS prices, access to a broader inventory base, and shortened lead times.
(62)      The input from most airline customers during the market investigation, generally
          confirmed that the claimed efficiencies are merger-specific and would benefit
          consumers (65), and that these efficiencies would likely materialise as a result of
          the Transaction. (66)
(63)      Nevertheless, at this stage the Commission did not conclude whether the merger
          specificity and consumer benefit are fulfilled, as it was in either case not in a
          position to conclude that the claimed efficiencies fulfil the verifiability criterion
          of the test. In particular, the evidence submitted by the Parties, while allowing for
          a preliminary consideration, would require further verification to assess their
          likelihood and magnitude. This would include reconciling certain internal
          documentation with the Parties’ submissions and confirming that the estimated
          customer benefits are representative rather than based on illustrative examples.
          Such verification was not possible during this Phase I investigation.

6.5.3.3. Verifiability
(70)     As regards verifiability, the Commission concludes that the Parties’ evidence is
         insufficient to verify the claimed efficiencies for the following reasons.
(71)     First, the Commission acknowledges that pool synergies and geographic footprint
         synergies were mentioned in internal documents by the Parties. (82) These internal
         documents suggest that the Parties indeed considered that such efficiencies can
         result from this Transaction. However, the Commission observes that the cost
         saving estimates do not match between different internal documents and to RFI
         replies submitted by the Parties. Thus, the Commission would need further
         information to reconcile the different cost estimates and to consider the claimed
         efficiency verifiable.
(72)     Second, the Commission notes that with regards to inventory cost savings,
         reduced transportation costs, and improved lead time the Parties have provided
         illustrative internal examples of cost savings that could arise from the
         efficiencies. (83) However, the Parties did not establish that these specific
         examples are representative, and therefore that these cost savings or, in case of
         improved lead times, the cost reduction of customers from less down-time can be
         extrapolated across the Parties activities. Instead, the Commission would need
         cost saving estimates which representatively captures cost savings across different
         customers and geographies and not only for specific examples to accept the
         claimed efficiency.
(73)     Third, in instances where cost saving estimates per aircraft were provided, the
         Commission would need underlying data, further explanations and underlying
         assumptions to verify the claimed efficiency. (84)
(74)     Fourth, regarding optimising pool management, the Parties did not provide
         estimates of cost savings for themselves or the customer cost savings from
         reduced downtime. Therefore, the Commission considers that this efficiency
         claim is not verifiable.
(75)     Finally, as to the environmental efficiencies, the Commission observes that the
         Parties did not provide any estimates of the expected reduction in transport-related
         emission but merely submitted evidence which indeed show that there is a
         growing trend among airlines to adhere to public decarbonisation strategies. In
         any event, given that transport-related emissions are directly associated with the
         above transport cost savings, for which it considers the efficiency not verifiable,
         the same conclusion would apply to environmental efficiencies.
